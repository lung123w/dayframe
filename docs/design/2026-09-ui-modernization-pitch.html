DayFrame UI/UX modernization — three directions, one recommendation

DayFrame's interface works, but it reads like a 2020 dashboard: a dark rail, 71 hex values, 13 type sizes, 1,828 lines of CSS belonging to screens nobody can reach, and a keyboard scorecard of 1 out of 5. This deck carries a code-grounded audit, three genuinely different directions, and the design stack I recommend adopting for each stage.

The two cheapest pillars to move are keyboard affordance and information hierarchy. Both are stylesheet-and-token work with zero data-model risk, and both drag the other four up with them. That single observation drives the recommendation in §7.

Friction in the daily loop (audit §4)

  • Capture: 3 pointer actions from cold open (rail → input → Enter); reachable only from Today, and the planner's own inbox cannot create anything.
  • Schedule: every path is HTML5 drag only — no button, no keyboard, no accessible name.
  • Execute: complete is 1 click (mouse only); habit logging is 3–4 interactions.
  • Review: the review opens on the week containing yesterday, names a different week in its header, and has no close-out step — the designed daily shutdown surface exists but is imported by nothing.

What the audit could not verify

  • No rendered screen was inspected — visual claims are read from source, not observed.
  • No contrast ratio was measured; the light-only concern (F15) is a statement about surfaces and use-time, not a WCAG result.
  • No usage data exists and none was invented; the click counts are read from handlers.
  • The live database was not queried; DB claims rest on .dev_context/DATA_MODEL.md.
  • Cost estimates are scope judgements, not schedules.

02Concept A

Calm Canvas

A day, not a document. Generous whitespace, hairlines instead of boxes, one recessive accent reserved for state, and a single capture line that is always there. The cheapest and lowest-risk of the three — and the one that departs least from your own Notion + Stripe reference.

Surface type: Decide / Monitor hybrid — a calm day list Cold open: Today (0 clicks) Accent: one, state-only Cost: S–M · 6–9 sessions · incremental

Layout idea

No permanent dark rail. A 52px top strip carries the app name, the day's date as the page title, and four text destinations plus a "More" overflow (Finance, Projects, Backup, Notifications). Below it one centred column — the day in reading order — separated by hairlines, no card backgrounds, no shadows. Each line: completion circle, title at body size, right-aligned metadata in one muted size. Hover or focus reveals exactly two text actions (move, defer). A ~260px habit rail collapses to "6 of 8 done" until clicked. The timeline becomes a thin vertical hours strip reading alongside the list, and the week a horizontal band of seven hairline-divided columns.

Navigation flow (cold open → "what do I do now")

  • Add a task — the capture line is the first element, always present: / to focus, type, Enter. New tasks inherit the last-used project.
  • Move a task onto today — in Week the backlog is a left band; each row carries an inline "Today" text action. One click. Drag still works.
  • Log a habit — one click on the habit row's completion control logs "done" with zero minutes; duration is an optional refinement.
  • Get to today's plan — it is the cold open: 0 clicks.

Key highlights

  • Today becomes the cold open (App.jsx:41); the planner becomes a reference surface, not a second planner.
  • Task rows replace task cards: hairlines, no border-left colour stripe, no card fill.
  • Hover/focus reveals become two persistent-in-focus text actions, so reorder and defer survive touch and keyboard (F10).
  • Habit rail logs in one click (F22); the shared manual-minutes input disappears (F23).
  • A three-size type ladder replaces 13 px values and 194 declarations in the 9–12px band (F05).
  • Saves become visible and debounced, one "Saved / Could not save" line (F25, F26); the dead day-select buttons start selecting days (F20).

03Concept B

Timeline Studio

Time becomes the primary axis. Drag a chip into an hour and it is scheduled and timed in one gesture; a persistent day lens answers "what now" without navigation. The most ambitious of the three and the biggest departure from your reference brief.

Surface type: Operate — a time canvas Cold open: this week, auto-scrolled to today Accent: one saturated now-line Cost: L · 14–20 sessions · a third of the front end is rebuilt

Layout idea

A dark canvas. A ~56px icon rail holds destinations; the main area is a genuine time axis — seven day columns with hour rows, so the week's shape is legible before you read a single title. Blocks are positioned and sized by time, tinted by project at low saturation. A ~280px left tray holds the unscheduled backlog as draggable chips; a ~64px bottom bar is the "day lens" (current hour, current block, start/complete/defer). Clicking a block opens an inline split editor instead of a modal. Habits become a bottom-left dot cluster. Empty hours stay empty — which is exactly the signal you need when deciding where something goes.

Navigation flow (cold open → "what do I do now")

  • Add a task — type in the always-visible tray input, Enter. One action; it appears as a chip.
  • Move it onto today — drag the chip into today's column at the intended hour: schedules and times it in one gesture.
  • Log a habit — click the habit's dot in the bottom-left cluster. One click; a second click opens duration entry.
  • Get to today's plan — it is the cold open at week zoom; the lens zoom gives a single day.

Key highlights

  • Drag-to-create: dragging on empty grid space creates a block with start and duration already set.
  • A drag ghost with a live time label, so the target hour is visible before release.
  • Keyboard parity for every drag: select, arrow-move, Enter-commit, undo.
  • The three competing time fields collapse into one model in the interface — but only after an ADR.
  • Habit logging becomes a dot matrix: eight clicks in one place instead of eight popover round trips.
  • Project colour finally means something: it is the block tint.

04Concept C

Command Deck

Raw speed. A command palette as the primary navigation, one capture surface on every view, keyboard operation everywhere, and tabular monospace numbers so estimates are comparable down the page. The smallest visual change of the three and the largest change to how the app is operated.

Surface type: Command / Operate Cold open: Today, cursor on the first unfinished item Accent: selection & commands only Cost: M · 9–13 sessions · incremental, two new pieces

Layout idea

A light, functional interface: a ~48px icon rail for destinations, one content pane, and no header restating where you already are. A permanent ~44px command bar sits at the bottom of the window, always typeable, showing the last action taken. The day is a dense ordered list — one line per task, with a monospace right column for numbers (estimate, time, project code) so durations line up. Above the list, a one-line "now" strip carries the current block, minutes remaining and the next item. Selection is a left rule plus a tint, never a card. Panels do not stack: a row expands in place; only genuinely modal work (recurrence, photos) opens a dialog.

Navigation flow (cold open → "what do I do now")

  • Add a task — Ctrl+K or / focuses the bar, type, Enter. From any view.
  • Move a task onto today — row selected, press t. One keystroke.
  • Log a habit — h lists habits in the palette; type a name, Enter logs it, +15 suffixes add duration.
  • Get to today's plan — g d, or palette → "today".

Key highlights

  • One palette reaches every destination, every task by fuzzy title, every habit by name and every action ("defer to tomorrow", "log 30 minutes guitar").
  • A single global capture line — the same control the palette uses, so there is one place to type.
  • j/k move, x complete, t defer, e edit in place, Ctrl+Z undo — replacing 12 hover-only icons.
  • Tabular right-aligned numbers in one mono column; all destructive actions route through one undo toast; the command bar reports "Saved 14:32".

Today's Escape handling is document-level, and the app's single tabIndex is on the finance photo paste target — a global key layer could silently steal that keystroke. Inline editing must also preserve the save semantics that already work (finance notes save on blur).

05

Recommended design stack

The audit's own conclusion is the starting constraint: the cheapest pillars to move are keyboard affordance and information hierarchy, and both are token-and-stylesheet work with no data-model risk. So the stack question is not "what is modern?" but "what moves those two pillars furthest per unit of risk?" — for a single-maintainer, single-user, local-first app that must stay usable while it changes.

Recommendation: a CSS-custom-property token layer first (no new dependency), then Radix UI primitives for the interaction shell. Defer Tailwind, shadcn/ui and Framer Motion, with explicit re-entry triggers.

Reason in one line: the token layer buys the consistency that a utility framework is usually bought for, and Radix buys the accessibility that is genuinely hard to hand-roll (focus trap and return, Escape, aria wiring) — while paying nothing for the parts of Tailwind/shadcn/Framer that DayFrame does not currently need.

PieceVerdictWhy, and what it costs
Token layer
src/styles/tokens.css		 Adopt now CSS custom properties for colour, type, space, radius, motion. One file, two mode sets (light + dark written together even if dark ships later). ~1 session, 0 dependencies, reversible by deleting one file. Immediately kills F03/F04/F05/F06/F07 at the root: 71 hexes, 13 sizes, 12 radii and 51 transition values become lists you can read.
Radix primitives
@radix-ui/react-dialog, -popover, -dropdown-menu, -tooltip, -tabs		 Adopt now Unstyled, so our CSS stays ours. Fixes the accessibility class of flaws that is hardest to hand-roll: focus trap and focus return, Escape, aria wiring, popover positioning (today's heat-map popover is positioned with no viewport clamping). Replaces window.confirm/window.alert/window.prompt (F27) and the hand-rolled modal/popovers. Cost: ~5 small packages, ~2 sessions for the modal+popover set, adopted per component as each surface is repainted.
Tailwind CSS v4 Defer Its win is consistency at scale — which the token layer already delivers here. The cost is a rewrite of className across 28 components and ~7,700 CSS lines in one move, which destroys the "one shippable slice per session" plan that makes this app safe to change while it is in daily use. Re-entry trigger: if the component count roughly triples, or a second maintainer joins. Migration, if it ever happens, is by co-existence, not big-bang.
shadcn/ui Defer Requires Tailwind, so it inherits the same objection, and it brings a component vocabulary we would then own and upgrade. Radix underneath gives most of the benefit without the styling opinions. Same re-entry trigger.
Framer Motion Defer The motion budget for Concept A is state change under ~150ms plus two overlays; CSS transitions plus a prefers-reduced-motion block cover it at 0 KB (today the app has 0 reduced-motion rules — F07). Adopt only if the timeline direction (B) is chosen, where drag ghosts, live time labels and block resizing genuinely need it.
Icon set Keep react-icons is already installed and used throughout; the audit's complaint (F39) is three emoji/dingbat glyphs used as chrome, not the icon library. Fix by replacing those three glyphs, not by adding a package.
Font deliverable Adopt now Stop loading Plus Jakarta Sans from Google Fonts (src/index.css:1) — a local-first app should not depend on the network for its own typography, and offline it silently reflows. Self-host one variable font (or use the system stack) and keep the file in the repo.

Recommendation

Recommended direction

Concept A — Calm Canvas

with Concept C's keyboard discipline folded in as an engineering addition, not as a second direction: a visible focus ring and a small documented shortcut set land in A's first two stages, and the command palette becomes A's natural last slice — it is the mechanism that finally makes "capture from anywhere" one keystroke.

Why A, in the audit's own numbers

  • The scorecard's lowest pillar is keyboard affordance (1/5), and four others sit at 2/5. A is the only direction that raises all six at once — and it does so on the cheapest change of the three.
  • It touches no data model, no route and no server code: every one of its stages is independently shippable, which is the precondition for changing the look of a tool that is in daily use.
  • It fixes, in priority order: the cold open (F-flow), hidden actions (F10), the habit ritual (F22), per-keystroke writes and invisible saves (F25/F26), the type ladder (F05), the palette split (F03/F04), the dead stylesheets (F11/F12) and the mobile dead end (F16) — and it gives the week bar back its day selection (F20) instead of designing around a control that does nothing.

What A does not fix — and why that is acceptable now

Time clarity. Without a real axis, "where does this 90-minute block actually fit" stays a judgement call (F18/F19). That is the strongest argument for Concept B — and B's price is a reconciliation of the three competing time fields plus a third of the front end rebuilt while the app is in use. So: keep B's timeline as the next initiative, gated behind an ADR on the time model, and do not pay for it now.

What happens after this deck

Decompose the choiceThe lead turns the chosen direction into an OpenSpec change plus atomic build cards — token layer first, then screen-by-screen — each with a verification card, and a guard that no screen may regress todayOrder, the Monday-start period windows, recurring overrides or the finance photos.

Honesty notes about this deck

  • No screen was rendered or screenshotted while producing the audit or the mockups above: the mockups are hand-built wireframes expressing layout intent, not captures of a real or prototyped UI. The dev server currently runs another lane's branch with uncommitted work, so it was deliberately not used as an evidence source.
  • The audit's own limits carry over: no contrast measurements, no usage data, no live-DB queries, and cost figures are scope judgements rather than schedules.
  • The four "dead" components may carry intent — DailyShutdown reads and writes daily_notes and looks unfinished rather than abandoned. Revive-or-delete is a product decision for the chosen direction.
  • The card that commissioned this work described "React 18" and "no icon package". Both are wrong: the app is React 19.2 with react-icons installed and used. The audit's §6 records it; nothing in this deck depends on the stale claim.
